3. GDP Cartogram

Circle area represents GDP. Initial circle positions are geographic centroids, then a collision force separates overlap while keeping countries near their approximate locations.

4. Design and Comparison

The choropleth and cartogram emphasize different questions even though they use the same GDP data. The choropleth preserves familiar country boundaries, locations, and neighborhood relationships, so it is especially useful for identifying where high-GDP economies are located and whether economic size shows geographic clustering. I use a logarithmic sequential color scale because the GDP distribution is strongly skewed; without transformation, the largest economies would dominate the color range and many other top-50 countries would look too similar.

The cartogram shifts the main visual channel from color to area. Economies such as the United States and China become visually dominant, while smaller economies shrink. This makes magnitude comparison more immediate, but geographic shape and precise spatial relationships are sacrificed. The Dorling-style layout keeps circles near geographic centroids, which preserves approximate regional context while avoiding overlapping symbols. Linked highlighting helps compensate for distortion: clicking or hovering over a country in either view emphasizes the same country in both. Overall, the choropleth better supports geographic-location and regional-pattern tasks, while the cartogram better supports direct comparison of economic magnitude.
